§ 01 — Setup

Why CommonsenseQA Is the Critical Test

CommonsenseQA tests everyday real-world reasoning — causality, spatial relationships, social norms, object properties. It is maximally different from GSM8K math. The guide was never trained on anything like this. If a base 3B model is going to help through sheer size and world knowledge, commonsense is where that argument is strongest. Instead, it is where the base guide fails hardest.

§ 05 — The Failure Mechanism

Confident Misdirection

The base guide's failure mode is not random degradation. It is a specific and consistent pattern: the unstructured 3B plan sounds plausible, confidently points in the wrong direction, and the solver follows it unanimously. The result is a tighter, more decisive ensemble that is wrong more often and wrong more confidently.

The Misdirection Pattern

Without fine-tuning, the 3B model generates verbose commonsense responses that read as reasonable but lack the explicit enumerate-and-eliminate structure that fine-tuning instills. When the solver reads a plan that says something like "knitting is a calming activity associated with focus and creativity" and commits to option D instead of A, all 5 votes follow the plan — producing unanimous confidence on a wrong answer. The baseline, without any plan, splits its votes more and occasionally votes correctly through simple pattern matching. The key insight: false decisiveness is worse than genuine uncertainty. A 3-2 split on a wrong answer gives a confidence signal of 0.60 — flagged as uncertain. A 5-0 split on a wrong answer gives a confidence signal of 1.0 — indistinguishable from a correct unanimous vote. The base guide converts 3-2 wrong splits into 5-0 wrong splits.

Wasted Votes vs False Confidence — The Trap

The base guide saves 34 wasted votes but adds 11 false confidence cases. Fewer wasted votes is normally positive — here it signals the solver has been made more decisively wrong, not more decisively right.

Questions at Maximum Confidence (5/5 votes agree)

Baseline: ~76% of questions at max confidence. Base guide: ~98%. Fine-tuned: 77% (N=100). The base guide collapses the entire ensemble to unanimous — regardless of whether it is right or wrong.
Why fine-tuning prevents this: GSM8K training teaches the model to enumerate explicit steps, eliminate wrong options by name, and commit to a specific answer with a stated reason. This structure forces the plan to be checkable — the solver can verify each step against the question. An unstructured plan ("knitting is calming and associated with relaxation and mindfulness, suggesting option A or D") leaves room for the solver to pick whichever letter it was already going to pick. A structured plan ("Step 2: Option B says arthritis — this is a possible outcome of repetitive motion, not the goal of knitting. Eliminate B. Step 3: Option A says relaxation — this matches the calming nature of knitting. Best answer: A") pins the solver to the correct reasoning chain.

What the CommonsenseQA Ablation Establishes

On the dataset most distant from the guide's training distribution, the base 3B model (no LoRA) actively degrades performance — −2.0 pts accuracy, 22% worse calibration, 11 more false-confidence failures, and 8 more total-failure questions at N=250. The failure mode is specific: unstructured plans make solver votes more decisive and more wrong simultaneously, converting uncertain 3-2 wrong splits into confident 5-0 wrong answers. This is not random noise — it is systematic confident misdirection. Fine-tuning reverses every metric: +9.0 pts accuracy, 33.7% better ECE, 31 fewer false-confidence cases. Across three ablation datasets — math, science, and commonsense — fine-tuned guidance is uniformly positive and base-3B guidance is inconsistent at best, harmful at worst. The LoRA adapter is not an optional enhancement. It is the mechanism that makes the pipeline work.
